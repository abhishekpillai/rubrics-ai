# Google re:Work: Structured Interviewing

Source: [Google re:Work - Use Structured Interviewing](https://rework.withgoogle.com/guides/hiring-use-structured-interviewing)

## Overview

Google's structured interviewing approach is the result of extensive internal research and validation. After analyzing hiring data across thousands of interviews, Google found that structured interviews were far more predictive of job performance than unstructured "conversational" interviews, while also reducing bias and improving candidate experience.

## Definition

**Structured interviewing** means using the same interviewing methods to assess all candidates applying for the same job. This includes:
- Asking identical questions to all candidates
- Training interviewers on proper techniques
- Scoring responses using consistent criteria
- Making hiring decisions based on predetermined qualifications

## The Four-Part Framework

Google's structured interview system has four key components:

### 1. Vetted, High-Quality Questions Relevant to the Role

**What this means:**
- Questions are pre-written and tested for job-relevance
- Each question maps to a specific competency or attribute
- Questions are job-specific, not generic
- **No brainteasers or puzzles** (Google explicitly abandoned these after finding no correlation with job performance)

**Best practices:**
- Create 2-3 questions per competency you're assessing
- Test questions with current high performers in the role
- Refresh questions regularly to prevent candidate preparation from external sources
- Ensure questions have clear, job-relevant purpose

**Question types to avoid:**
- Brainteasers ("How many golf balls fit in a school bus?")
- Generic personality questions ("What's your biggest weakness?")
- Leading questions that telegraph the desired answer
- Questions unrelated to job performance

### 2. Comprehensive Feedback Documentation

**What this means:**
- Interviewers take detailed notes on what candidates actually say and do
- Notes focus on evidence, not conclusions or impressions
- Documentation enables independent review by hiring committees
- Records allow for bias detection and interviewer calibration

**Best practices:**
- Document specific examples and quotes from the candidate
- Note both strengths and areas of concern with evidence
- Avoid interpretive language ("seemed nervous" → "paused 5+ seconds before answering")
- Write notes assuming others will review them without context

**Why this matters:**
Google found that separating evidence collection (interviewer role) from decision-making (hiring committee role) significantly reduced bias. When interviewers know their notes will be reviewed by others, they focus on observable facts rather than gut feelings.

### 3. Standardized Rubrics for Scoring

**What this means:**
- All interviewers use the same definition of poor, mediocre, good, and excellent answers
- Rubrics describe what each rating level looks like in behavioral terms
- Scoring is based on evidence against rubric, not interviewer preference
- Rubrics are specific to each question/competency

**Rubric structure:**
Each question should have a scoring guide that defines performance levels:

```
POOR (1-2 points)
- Behavioral indicator 1
- Behavioral indicator 2
- What this looks like: [example]

MIXED (3 points)
- Behavioral indicator 1
- Behavioral indicator 2
- What this looks like: [example]

GOOD (4 points)
- Behavioral indicator 1
- Behavioral indicator 2
- What this looks like: [example]

EXCELLENT (5 points)
- Behavioral indicator 1
- Behavioral indicator 2
- What this looks like: [example]
```

**Best practices:**
- Use observable behaviors, not adjectives or feelings
- Include concrete examples for each level
- Define what differentiates each level clearly
- Test rubrics with actual candidate responses to calibrate

**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- Vague language ("shows good judgment")
- Personality descriptions rather than behaviors
- Overlapping definitions between levels
- No clear behavioral anchors

### 4. Interviewer Training and Calibration

**What this means:**
- Interviewers are trained on the structured process before conducting interviews
- Training covers question asking, note-taking, and rubric usage
- Interviewers practice scoring together to ensure consistency
- Regular calibration sessions maintain alignment over time

**Training should include:**
- Rationale for structured interviewing (the research)
- How to ask questions and follow-ups without leading
- Note-taking techniques that capture evidence
- How to use rubrics to score consistently
- Common biases and how to mitigate them
- Practice interviews with feedback

**Calibration process:**
- Interviewers watch/read the same candidate responses
- Each scores independently using the rubric
- Group discusses any score discrepancies
- Aligns on what each rating level means in practice
- Repeats until interviewers converge on scoring

**Why this matters:**
Google research showed that even with structured questions and rubrics, interviewer variability was high without proper training. Calibration ensures all interviewers are using the same standards.

## Question Types Recommended by Google

### Behavioral Questions
Ask candidates to describe past experiences and behavior patterns.

**Format:** "Tell me about a time when..."

**Why they work:**
- Past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior
- Harder to fabricate than hypothetical responses
- Reveals actual experience and decision-making process

**Examples:**
- "Tell me about a time when you had to make a decision with incomplete information. What did you do?"
- "Describe a situation where you had to influence someone who disagreed with you. How did you approach it?"
- "Give me an example of when you had to prioritize competing demands. How did you decide?"

**Follow-up questions:**
- "What were you thinking at that moment?"
- "What alternatives did you consider?"
- "What would you do differently knowing what you know now?"
- "What was the outcome?"

### Hypothetical/Situational Questions
Present job-related scenarios to assess judgment and problem-solving.

**Format:** "What would you do if..."

**Why they work:**
- Useful when candidates lack direct experience
- Assesses reasoning and judgment
- Can present role-specific scenarios

**Examples:**
- "Imagine two team members fundamentally disagree on the technical approach. What would you do?"
- "You're three weeks from launch and discover a critical bug. Walk me through your thinking."
- "A stakeholder requests a feature that conflicts with your product strategy. How would you handle it?"

**Follow-up questions:**
- "What factors would you consider?"
- "What are the tradeoffs of that approach?"
- "How would you know if your decision was right?"

### What Google Explicitly Avoids

**Brainteasers (Abandoned):**
- "How many golf balls fit in a school bus?"
- "Why are manhole covers round?"

**Why:** Google's own research found zero correlation between brainteaser performance and job performance. They were evaluating "performance under pressure in an artificial scenario" rather than job-relevant skills.

**Self-Assessment Questions:**
- "What are your strengths and weaknesses?"
- "Rate yourself 1-10 on [skill]"

**Why:** Research shows self-ratings have no predictive validity. People are poor at assessing their own abilities, and answers are easily gamed.

## Google's Four Hiring Attributes

While the specific competencies vary by role, Google evaluates all candidates on four core attributes:

### 1. General Cognitive Ability
**What it is:** Ability to learn, process information, and solve problems

**Not the same as:** IQ, formal education, or academic pedigree

**How it's assessed:**
- How candidates approach unfamiliar problems
- Whether they can break down complex issues
- Learning from feedback during the interview

### 2. Emergent Leadership
**What it is:** Stepping up to lead when needed, stepping back when others should lead

**Not the same as:** Being extroverted, having management experience, or dominating conversations

**How it's assessed:**
- Examples of influencing without authority
- Knowing when to let others lead
- Taking responsibility when things go wrong

### 3. Role-Related Knowledge
**What it is:** Technical or functional expertise needed for the specific job

**Not the same as:** Years of experience, certifications, or educational background

**How it's assessed:**
- Depth of knowledge in relevant areas
- Ability to apply knowledge to novel scenarios
- Current understanding of the field

### 4. Googleyness (Cultural Alignment)
**What it is:** Comfort with ambiguity, bias toward action, collaborative mindset

**Not the same as:** "Culture fit" or "someone I'd have a beer with"

**How it's assessed:**
- How candidates handle ambiguous situations
- Examples of taking initiative
- Collaboration and teamwork behaviors

**Important:** Google explicitly defines these attributes to avoid vague "culture fit" assessments that perpetuate bias. Each attribute has behavioral questions and rubrics.

## Research-Backed Benefits

Google's internal research validated through external studies shows:

### Higher Predictive Validity
Structured interviews are more predictive of actual job performance than unstructured interviews.

**Internal data:** Correlation between interview scores and job performance was significantly higher with structured approach.

### Time Savings
**~40 minutes saved per interview** from using pre-made questions, guides, and rubrics rather than creating ad-hoc questions.

### Reduced Bias
**"Decreased differences between demographic groups"** in hiring outcomes when using structured interviews.

### Improved Candidate Experience
**35% higher satisfaction** among rejected candidates who had structured vs. unstructured interviews.

**Why this matters:** Rejected candidates are potential customers, future applicants, and sources of referrals. A positive experience even in rejection strengthens employer brand.

### Perceived Fairness
Candidates rated structured interviews as fairer and more legitimate than unstructured conversations, even when the outcome was rejection.

## Implementation Challenges and Solutions

### Challenge 1: "But structured interviews feel robotic"

**The concern:** Interviewers worry that reading questions and taking notes will create an unnatural, uncomfortable experience.

**Google's response:**
- Build rapport in the introduction before structured questions begin
- Explain the process to candidates upfront ("I'll be taking notes for consistency")
- Use predetermined follow-ups that feel conversational
- Training helps interviewers deliver questions naturally

**Data:** Candidate experience data shows structured interviews are rated *higher* in satisfaction, not lower.

### Challenge 2: "I'm a good judge of talent - I don't need a script"

**The concern:** Experienced interviewers believe their intuition is accurate.

**Google's response:**
- Research shows even experts are overconfident in unstructured interviews
- Confirmation bias, halo effect, and similarity bias affect everyone
- Google's own data showed experienced interviewers were not more accurate without structure
- Structured approaches augment expertise, don't replace it

**Quote from research:** "Interviewers everywhere think they're good at interviewing, but the data rarely supports this."

### Challenge 3: "Creating questions and rubrics takes too long"

**The concern:** Building a structured interview system requires significant upfront investment.

**Google's response:**
- Upfront time investment pays off in time saved per interview (~40 min)
- Questions and rubrics can be reused across multiple hiring cycles
- Cross-functional collaboration can distribute the work
- Starting with one role and iterating is better than not starting

**ROI:** Time breaks even after ~3-5 interviews, then saves time on every subsequent interview.

### Challenge 4: "What about unique candidates who don't fit the mold?"

**The concern:** Structured interviews might miss exceptional candidates with unconventional backgrounds.

**Google's response:**
- Competency-based questions assess ability, not background
- Behavioral questions allow candidates to draw from any experience
- Rubrics evaluate quality of response, not specific credentials
- Structure reduces bias *against* unconventional candidates

**Data:** Structured interviews actually *increase* diversity in hiring outcomes.

## Practical Implementation Guide

### Phase 1: Define Competencies (Week 1-2)
1. Analyze the job description
2. Interview current high performers
3. Identify 3-5 critical competencies
4. Define each competency behaviorally

### Phase 2: Develop Questions (Week 2-3)
1. Create 2-3 questions per competency
2. Mix behavioral and situational questions
3. Write predetermined follow-ups
4. Pilot test with friendly candidates

### Phase 3: Build Rubrics (Week 3-4)
1. Define 4 performance levels per question
2. Use behavioral anchors, not adjectives
3. Include example responses for each level
4. Review with hiring managers and interviewers

### Phase 4: Train Interviewers (Week 4-5)
1. Explain the research and rationale
2. Walk through questions and rubrics
3. Practice with mock interviews
4. Calibrate scoring as a group
5. Address concerns and adjust

### Phase 5: Launch and Iterate (Week 6+)
1. Conduct first structured interviews
2. Gather feedback from interviewers and candidates
3. Track scores and outcomes
4. Calibrate regularly (monthly initially)
5. Refresh questions quarterly

## Integration with Hiring Committee Model

Google separates interviewers from decision-makers through a hiring committee model:

**Interviewers:**
- Conduct structured interviews
- Document evidence (what candidate said/did)
- Score using rubrics
- Submit feedback without recommendation

**Hiring Committee:**
- Reviews feedback from all interviewers
- Looks for patterns across interviews
- Makes hiring decision based on aggregate data
- Can identify interviewer bias/aberrations

**Benefits:**
- Reduces individual interviewer bias
- Prevents "loudest voice" dominating decisions
- Ensures consistent standards across teams
- Creates accountability through peer review

## Measuring Success

Google recommends tracking:

### Process Metrics
- Interviewer calibration scores (inter-rater reliability)
- Time to complete interviews
- Candidate satisfaction scores
- Quality of interviewer notes

### Outcome Metrics
- Interview score vs. job performance correlation
- Offer acceptance rates
- First-year retention rates
- Performance ratings of hires
- Adverse impact ratios across demographic groups

### Continuous Improvement
- Review metrics quarterly
- Update questions showing poor predictive validity
- Recalibrate interviewers showing score drift
- Gather qualitative feedback from candidates and interviewers

## Key Takeaways

1. **Structure beats intuition:** Decades of research and Google's own data show structured interviews are more accurate and fair than unstructured conversations.

2. **Four components are essential:** Questions, documentation, rubrics, and training all work together. Skipping any component reduces effectiveness.

3. **Bias reduction requires active effort:** Structure helps, but interviewers must be trained to recognize and mitigate bias.

4. **Candidate experience improves:** Despite concerns, candidates rate structured interviews as fairer and more satisfying.

5. **Upfront investment pays off:** Time spent developing structure is repaid through faster, better hiring decisions.

6. **Measure and iterate:** Track outcomes, gather feedback, and continuously improve the system.
